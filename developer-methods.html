Methodology

What each reading on these pages means, where it comes from, and what it can't tell you. The public numbers behind every report are explained on the open site's Methods page; this page covers only what the developer pages add.

1. The Where Should I Build? score

Where Should I Build? scores every town, Census community and county in the needs ranking from 0 to 100. Each factor earns 0 to 10 points by a written rule, and you set how much each group counts. The rule and source for every factor are printed on that page under "How the score works".

  • Score = the weighted points of the checked factors as a share of their maximum, times 100. Dated public events from the last 12 months add up to +10. Red flags take off up to 30.
  • Not yet checked is never a zero. The factor is left out and confidence drops instead.
  • Confidence is the share of your weight that rests on checked factors: High 80% or more, Medium 50 to 79%, Low under 50%.
  • Tier: Target at 70 or more (not with Low confidence), Promising at 50, Monitor at 35.

Jobs within reach (added 29 October 2026) counts every Colorado job within a 30-minute drive of where the town's working residents live, its own jobs included (LEHD LODES 2023; drive times on OpenStreetMap roads). 1,000 jobs or fewer is 0 points and 100,000 or more is 10, on a log scale. It is off in the smaller-town preset so remote towns are not marked down. Commuters still count toward need only in the town where they work, so a bedroom town is not credited with its neighbor's need.

Workforce commuting in (added 30 October 2026) is the share of a place's primary jobs held by workers paid $3,333 a month or less who live 20 to 60 straight-line miles away (LEHD LODES 2023). 2% or less is 0 points and 20% or more is 10. Places with fewer than 500 primary jobs are not checked, and trips of 60 miles or more are left out because LODES puts some workers at a head office. It is the mirror of jobs within reach: it rewards the town workers drive in to, not the town they live in. So it is 0 in every preset and moves a score only when you turn it up. The figure shows on every row either way.

2. Which places are in the pipeline

A place enters the pipeline when its Opportunity Index from the Opportunity Finder is above about 60 out of 100. The index blends five parts: housing need, how recently the place won tax credits, the basis boost, population and civic readiness. Their weights change with the deal type you pick, and the Finder prints them under its methodology. A higher score means the public record points to a place that can support a tax-credit deal. It doesn't mean anyone there has asked for one.

The pipeline stages (Screen, Signal, Outreach and on) and the A to D classes are defined on the Pipeline page.

3. The readiness reading: ripe, ripening, not yet

A reading comes only from a site dossier. Each dossier answers seven questions with a source for each answer:

  1. Is there need?
  2. Is there a path to site control?
  3. Are the entitlements firm?
  4. Does a deal close?
  5. Is there local support?
  6. Is a funding window within 12 months?
  7. Are hard blockers cleared?

Each answer is yes, partly or not yet, and cites the dossier section it comes from. From those answers the dossier sets one reading, ripe, ripening or not yet, and names the one thing that would change it, with a date when there is one. A site without a dossier shows as "on file" and gets no reading.

Every label links to the award list, minutes or news story it rests on. Land held by a public owner with no developer attached ranks first, because it is where pggLLC can offer a solution without stepping on anyone's work.

5. "Call first" on the place card

The three people shown are ranked against the place's next step, in this order:

  1. Fit to the next step. A zoning question goes to planning, a land or request-for-proposals question to the housing authority or landholder, a funding vote to the manager or council.
  2. A phone or email on file.
  3. A logged talk. Between people who fit the step equally and are equally easy to reach, anyone you've talked to (per the Discussions log) comes first.
  4. The relationship you marked. Only if all three are still tied, a contact you marked as a strong or warm relationship in your network file comes first.

Each pick says why it was chosen. A person found in research shows only with a public source link, such as a staff directory or meeting minutes.

6. Dossiers

A dossier is written only where the place scores above 60 and has a parcel that suits a tax-credit deal. Land held for housing and large entitled lots come first. Every dossier's comparable sales or projects carry a map, an address and a link to the public record for each one.

7. Sources and limits

Everything shown comes from public records: Census and HUD data, state award lists, county parcel layers and assessor records, and town and county minutes. The open site's About page lists the core sources. Your own notes, contacts and the Discussions log are saved only on this developer site.

Public records lag. A council can act between agendas, and a developer can drop a site without saying so. Treat every reading as the starting point for a call, not a decision.
