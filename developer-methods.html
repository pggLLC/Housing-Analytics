Methodology

What each reading on these pages means, where it comes from, and what it can't tell you. The public numbers behind every report are explained on the open site's Methods page; this page covers only what the developer pages add.

1. Which places are in the pipeline

A place enters the pipeline when its Opportunity Index from the Opportunity Finder is above about 60 out of 100. The index blends need, local funding, civic capacity, basis boost and market room; its parts are set out on Methods: the score. A higher score means the public record points to a place that can support a tax-credit deal. It doesn't mean anyone there has asked for one.

The pipeline stages (Screen, Signal, Outreach and on) and the A to D classes are defined on the Pipeline page.

2. The readiness reading: ripe, ripening, not yet

A reading comes only from a site dossier. Each dossier answers seven questions with a source for each answer:

  1. Is there need?
  2. Is there a path to site control?
  3. Are the entitlements firm?
  4. Does a deal close?
  5. Is there local support?
  6. Is a funding window within 12 months?
  7. Are hard blockers cleared?

Each answer is yes, partly or not yet, and cites the dossier section it comes from. From those answers the dossier sets one reading, ripe, ripening or not yet, and names the one thing that would change it, with a date when there is one. A site without a dossier shows as "on file" and gets no reading.

3. The developer check on each site

Before a site is suggested, public records are checked for who is already on it:

LabelWhat it means
OpenA public owner (a town, county or housing authority) holds the land and hasn't chosen a developer.
Developer searchA request for proposals is open or announced.
OpeningThe last applicant appears to have moved on.
UnclearNo developer is named in the public record.
TakenA developer is still applying or building. These sites stay listed so you know, but are never suggested.

Every label links to the award list, minutes or news story it rests on. Land held by a public owner with no developer attached ranks first, because it is where pggLLC can offer a solution without stepping on anyone's work.

4. "Call first" on the place card

The three people shown are ranked against the place's next step:

  1. Fit to the next step. A zoning question goes to planning, a land or request-for-proposals question to the housing authority or landholder, a funding vote to the manager or council.
  2. A phone or email on file.
  3. A logged talk. Anyone you've talked to, per the Discussions log, rises above a cold contact.

Each pick says why it was chosen. A person found in research shows only with a public source link, such as a staff directory or meeting minutes.

5. Dossiers

A dossier is written only where the place scores above 60 and has a parcel that suits a tax-credit deal. Land held for housing and large entitled lots come first. Every dossier's comparable sales or projects carry a map, an address and a link to the public record for each one.

6. Sources and limits

Everything shown comes from public records: Census and HUD data, state award lists, county parcel layers and assessor records, and town and county minutes. The open site's About page lists the core sources. Your own notes, contacts and the Discussions log are saved only on this developer site.

Public records lag. A council can act between agendas, and a developer can drop a site without saying so. Treat every reading as the starting point for a call, not a decision.
