Federal Rule Changes That Would Help Affordable Housing

What Congress and federal agencies could change to aim housing money at the households with the least, lower the cost of borrowing, and cut the overhead that eats into gap financing. Fourteen changes, ranked, each tied to the law or rule it would change.

Policy brief · Current as of 9 November 2026 · Sources checked 6 to 9 November 2026. Laws, rules and funding levels change; check the linked source before relying on a figure.

The short answer

Congress and HUD already did a lot in the last 16 months, so any proposal should start from current law, not 2024 wish lists. The 2025 tax law made the 12% tax credit increase permanent and cut the bond test from 50% to 25%. The ROAD to Housing Act (Public Law 119-101, law on 11 July 2026) killed the HOME 24-month commitment deadline, exempted some HOME work from NEPA, made new construction a CDBG use, and let tax credit inspections count for vouchers. Most of the ROAD Act still needs HUD rules, and it appropriates no money.

The changes below are ranked by how much they would move deals, then by how hard they are to get. "Admin" means HUD, Treasury, USDA or FHFA can do it without Congress.

1. Targeting help at those in most need

What the law does now

  • Income averaging lets a tax credit building mix 20% to 80% AMI units if the average stays at 60%: "The average of the imputed income limitations designated ... shall not exceed 60 percent of area median gross income" and "shall be 20 percent, 30 percent, 40 percent, 50 percent, 60 percent, 70 percent, or 80 percent" (26 U.S.C. 42(g)(1)(C)). The 2025 final procedures remove the risk that one noncompliant unit costs the whole building its credits.
  • The Housing Trust Fund is the only federal capital program aimed at the poorest renters. While the national total is under $1 billion, "the grantee must use 100 percent of its HTF grant for the benefit of extremely low-income families" (24 CFR 93.250). The statute's floor is 75% (12 U.S.C. 4568). HUD's FY2027 budget shows $195.0M appropriated for 2026 and an estimated $267M for 2027 (HUD 2027 CJ, HTF). Novogradac reports the 2026 allocation at $255.5M, announced 3 Aug 2026 after a delay (secondary; Novogradac, 2 Sep 2026).
  • HTF and the Capital Magnet Fund both come from a 4.2 basis-point set-aside on Fannie and Freddie new business, 65% to HTF and 35% to CMF (12 U.S.C. 4567).
  • The HOME rule now lets an owner take the full rental-assistance payment even if it runs above the HOME rent (CRS R48422). That is the main federal tool for pairing deep rents with vouchers.

Changes that would help

  1. More HTF money, protected. Raising the 4.2 bp set-aside is the cleanest route to more deep-rent units without new appropriations. Any plan to end Fannie and Freddie conservatorship should carry the set-aside forward. Unconfirmed: current status of any conservatorship-exit plan; not checked for this note.
  2. An extremely low-income basis boost in the tax credit. This was in the Affordable Housing Credit Improvement Act. It was not in the 2025 tax law and is still only a bill (H.R. 2725, S. 1515). We did not re-read the bill text for this note; confirm the boost's terms before citing them.
  3. Pair vouchers with tax credit units by default. The ROAD Act inspection change (§405) removes one barrier. HUD can go further by letting PHAs attach project-based vouchers through the HFA's tax credit round, so one competition covers both.
  4. Restore the HOME 270% subsidy cap. HUD's 2025 rule raised the cap in high-cost areas to 270% of the Section 234 limits, then a 2026 notice reverted it to 240% (CRS R48422). Deep-rent units need more subsidy per unit, so a lower cap pushes HOME toward shallower targeting.

2. Cutting cost with rate programs like Wyoming's

What Wyoming actually did, checked against the Legislature's own records

Wyoming lawmakers have offered four versions of this idea in three sessions. None became law:

Year Bill What it would do What happened
2024 Budget amendment HB0001H3005 Treasurer may invest up to $15M a year from the Permanent Wyoming Mineral Trust Fund reserve in WCDA bonds, for first-time buyer loans "at a mortgage interest rate not to exceed three and one-half percent (3.5%)" (amendment) Not in the enacted budget. The 2024 budget act (Ch. 118) has no such footnote; we searched it for "homebuy" and "mortgage."
2024 HB0221 Same idea as a stand-alone bill "Did not Consider for Introduction," 16 Feb 2024 (wyoleg.gov)
2025 HB0067 Treasurer "shall invest ... a sum not to exceed sixty million dollars ($60,000,000.00)" from the mineral trust fund in WCDA bonds for veterans and first-time buyers "at a rate that is two percent (2%) below the available mortgage rate," sunset 1 July 2028 (bill) "Did not Consider for Introduction," 3 Feb 2025 (wyoleg.gov)
2026 SF0064 $30M Wyoming housing revolving loan program run by WCDA. Loans to towns, housing authorities and nonprofits for new building, rehab and land, at a rate that "shall not exceed the United States effective federal funds rate" (bill) "Failed Introduction 15-15," 10 Feb 2026 (wyoleg.gov; Wyoming Public Media, 16 Mar 2026)

What Wyoming does run is the normal state housing agency model. WCDA's deputy director told the Senate Banking subcommittee in 2024 that "WCDA receives no state funding" and that its "largest housing program is the Single-Family Mortgage Purchase Program for first-time homebuyers" (testimony, 15 Apr 2024). WCDA also used the 2009-10 federal New Issue Bond Program: "WCDA issued $70 million of bonds under the newly created New Issue Bond Program" (WCDA 2025 annual report).

So the "Wyoming model" is a proposal, not a program: use a state permanent fund to buy housing agency bonds at a set discount below market. The 2025 fiscal note shows the cost to the state. Permanent fund yields were projected near 3%, with long-run total returns of 6.75% (fiscal note). The cost is the return the trust fund gives up.

A neighbor that did pass it: Montana HB 819 (2023)

  • Board of Investments linked deposits: "Banks may request BOI deposits to reduce construction cost for housing developments." One example: Bigfork senior housing construction loan cut from 8.00% to 5.00% on $3.6M (BOI update, 12 Dec 2023).
  • Infrastructure bonds: the state "can purchase up to 50% of public infrastructure bonds at 25% of market rate." There is also a 0% impact-fee loan pool (same source).
  • Coal Trust Multifamily loans: "Section 16 of HB 819 appropriated $50 million to the Board of Housing" for low-rate loans to rental projects. Fifteen 2023-24 approvals are listed (Commerce, 16 Sep 2024).

What a rate cut is worth (our arithmetic, illustrative). On a 35-year loan, the same rent income that carries $1.00M at 6.5% carries about $1.28M at 4.5% and about $1.46M at 3.5%. A 2-point cut adds roughly 28% to the debt a deal can carry, and that comes straight off the gap. This is a fixed-payment calculation, not a model of any one deal.

Federal versions, ranked

  1. USDA Section 538 interest credit (in rule now). "For at least 20 percent of the loans made during each fiscal year, the Agency will provide assistance in the form of interest credit, to the extent necessary to reduce the agreed-upon rate of interest to the AFR" (7 CFR 3565.211). It is limited by money: "authority to ... provide interest credits is limited to the extent that appropriations are available" (§3565.4). FY2026 levels: 538 guarantees $400M, 515 direct loans $50M, 521 rental assistance $1,715M (Housing Assistance Council, secondary). Unconfirmed: whether the FY2026 538 notice offers interest credit.
  2. USDA Section 515 at 1%. "The Agency will provide interest credit to subsidize the interest on the Agency loan to a payment rate of 1 percent" (7 CFR 3560). At $50M a year, it funds a handful of deals nationally.
  3. FHA-HFA Risk Sharing with FFB money. FFB financed "nearly $4 billion (adjusted for inflation) in loans for 276 multifamily projects" that "produced or preserved about 30,900 rental units" from FY2016 to FY2025. GAO reports that in its FY2027 budget HUD "did not project that any new firm commitments would be issued in 2027 but stated it would honor existing commitments." NCSHA backs the program and the Mortgage Bankers Association opposes it (GAO-26-109028, 15 Sep 2026). Keeping it alive is an admin choice.
  4. Treasury bond purchase (NIBP 2.0). In 2009-10, the program "allowed the HFAs to issue $15.3 billion of bonds that were purchased by the Treasury through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac," with over 90 HFAs taking part (Bond Buyer, 13 Jan 2010, secondary). This is the federal form of the Wyoming bills. It would need new purchase authority, because the 2008 emergency authority it used has expired. Unconfirmed: exact legal authority now needed; check with bond counsel.
  5. Fannie and Freddie caps. For 2026, each GSE's multifamily cap is $88B, "at least 50 percent ... mission-driven, affordable housing," and "multifamily loans that finance workforce housing will be excluded from the 2026 limits" (FHFA, 24 Nov 2025). FHFA could price affordable loans below the regular grid, not just exclude them from the cap.
  6. Federal Home Loan Banks. By law the Affordable Housing Program gets "10 percent of the preceding year's net income" (12 U.S.C. 1430(j)(5)(C)). Raising that share, or using FHLBank advances the way Montana uses linked deposits, would cut construction rates through local banks.

3A. Small federal dollars that "federalize" a project

The problem. A deal financed mostly with tax credits, private debt and state money takes on federal rules when even a small HUD award is added. Each rule has its own trigger, and none of them scales with the size of the federal share:

Rule What triggers it Source quote
BABA Any HUD-funded housing project of 5 or more units, unless a waiver applies "Projects with one- to four-units should be classified as private and not subject to BABA. Housing projects with five or more units should be considered as public infrastructure subject to BABA unless another BABA waiver or exemption applies." (HUD Notice CPD-25-01, 13 Jan 2025)

The cost evidence is thin, but it points one way. The AP reported on 3 April 2026 that BABA "has caused construction delays and hundreds of thousands of dollars in extra costs." A Vermont developer spent "an additional $150,000 just to verify iron and steel." A Denver developer spent over $60,000 on a consultant to find U.S.-made materials and was still waiting on waivers for about 125 materials in an 85-unit building. A Kentucky developer plans two four-unit projects instead of one 20-to-30-unit project to stay under BABA. HUD "is taking at least six months to approve many waivers." NCSHA told the AP there is no national data on how much BABA adds (AP via Philadelphia Inquirer, 3 Apr 2026, secondary). The Fort Collins waiver in section 3 is the Colorado example. No study we found prices Davis-Bacon, Section 3 or NEPA compliance per unit for small HUD awards. Treat any per-unit figure as unconfirmed.

Congress has already written one lighter program. The Housing Trust Fund statute (12 U.S.C. 4568) and rule (24 CFR 93) contain no Davis-Bacon or NEPA requirement; we searched both texts. HTF instead sets its own environmental standards in the rule, such as historic preservation at 24 CFR 93.301(f). That is a working model for federal money with fixed standards instead of a federal process.

What a fix would look like

  1. BABA, admin (HUD and OMB). Change the small-project waiver test from total project cost (now up to $350,000) to the HUD share. HUD's notice ties this to the federal simplified acquisition threshold, which rose from $250,000 to $350,000 on 1 Oct 2025 (FAR inflation rule, 27 Aug 2025), so the limit should now be $350,000. HUD has not published an updated figure that we found. For example, waive BABA where HUD money is under $1M or under 10% of total development cost. Extend both waivers past 23 November 2027, and issue standing nonavailability waivers for common housing products so each project does not apply separately. The ROAD Act review (§501, due 7 Jan 2027) is the opening. The $1M and 10% figures are our illustration, not a cited proposal.
  2. Section 3, admin (HUD rule). Raise the $200,000 project threshold, or measure it against the HUD share of cost.
  3. Davis-Bacon, Congress. Apply it to HOME or CDBG only when the federal share passes a set dollar amount or percentage, and raise the unit triggers (section 3).
  4. NEPA, already moving. The ROAD Act HOME exemptions and §206 exclusions cover most small infill deals once HUD's rules are final. Ask HUD to add a "minor federal share" exclusion in the same rulemaking.
  5. Automatic waivers for products not made in the U.S. (admin, HUD). BABA already lets an agency waive the rule where products "are not produced in the United States in sufficient and reasonably available quantities or of a satisfactory quality" (HUD Fort Collins waiver, quoting §70914(b)). HUD uses that power only project by project: its BABA page lists no general nonavailability waiver for any product (HUD BABA page, checked 6 Nov 2026). Other agencies have issued product-wide waivers. EPA's approved list includes a "General Applicability Non-Availability Waiver Build America, Buy America Product Waiver: AMI Water Meters," running 12/20/24 to 12/19/27 (EPA approved waivers). HUD could do the same for housing. It would publish one list of products already shown not to be made in the U.S., built from the project waivers it has granted, such as the eight packages in Fort Collins. Any HUD-funded project could then use those products without its own 15-day posting and review. It would refresh the list each year. Proposal; HUD has not proposed it. One caution: transportation is moving the other way. FHWA's manufactured-products waiver dated from 1983, and FHWA is phasing it out from 1 October 2025 (Venable, 16 Sep 2025, secondary).
  6. One statutory de minimis clause. A single section saying that HUD assistance below a set amount or share of cost does not by itself trigger BABA, Davis-Bacon or Section 3 would fix the problem in one place instead of program by program. Proposal only; no bill with this text was found.

Program use About $208M of 538 commitments expected for FY2027, below the program level; the source calls it a program that "uses half its authority." MMCG, Fall 2026 monitor, 1 Oct 2026 (secondary)

What is problematic, in USDA's own words. In the 25 Sep 2026 notice, USDA said stakeholders "identified challenges that include a lack of clarity around Agency requirements, processes, and timelines; inconsistencies and delays in Agency third-party report reviews; and duplicative or unnecessary Agency application requirements that increase costs and waste time." It also said third-party reports and their review "may add significant time and expense."

538 no longer runs on a yearly funding round. Since 14 Nov 2019, USDA has accepted applications "on a continuous basis," because a late notice could land "after the LIHTC application deadline," and then "the developer will not be able to use LIHTC" (84 FR 55035, 15 Oct 2019). Money is still obligated only while appropriations last, and ties go by priority score and then the order USDA approved them (2024 notice).

What USDA already changed in 2026.

  • The pilot (9 Oct 2026 to 25 Sep 2028), for the first 200 538 loans only: the debt coverage test drops to 1.11. Lenders need only borrower financial statements, with certified statements "if requested." Where a deal also has tax credits or HUD money, USDA "may defer to the applicable Federal or State program requirements where doing so reduces duplicative administration." Paperwork is tiered by risk. Qualified lenders and nonprofits can process low-risk transfers. USDA "may accept recent HUD environmental review documentation, capital needs assessments, or related third-party reports" for pilot properties "that also receive HUD financing, subsidy, insurance, or recapitalization support." For 515 transfers only, projects can follow state and local codes "instead of relying on the standards in 7 CFR part 1924." USDA will track "processing times" and "report costs" and says it will change its rules at the end (91 FR 60930).
  • Loan size: the maximum loan-to-cost for the continuous guarantee rises from 70% to 80% (same notice). Industry had asked for 90%, a 1.11 coverage test and 50-year amortization (CARH, 28 Mar 2025).
  • Fees, from 30 Sep 2026: the standard fee stays at 65 basis points upfront and 35 a year. USDA dropped the reduced fee for green buildings, citing Executive Order 14148, and added a reduced fee (60 and 25) for Opportunity Zones (USDA notice, 30 Sep 2026). That is one more energy incentive gone (see section 3C).
  • HUD–USDA environmental agreement, signed 29 Sep 2026: it commits the two to "develop a process to designate a lead agency" and to study shared inspections. It does not yet let one agency rely on the other's review. One summary put it this way: "Nothing in this document makes a closing faster this fiscal year" (Granted, 1 Oct 2026, secondary; primary MOU not found).
  • Pending: a June 2025 proposed rule would require a market study for every new construction 538 application (90 FR, 30 Jun 2025). It was not final as of October 2026 (MMCG, secondary). The Rural Housing Service Reform Act (H.R. 4957, introduced 12 Aug 2025 by Reps. Nunn and Cleaver) would authorize money for Rural Housing Service staff and technology and require a yearly program report (text). No record of passage was found.

3C. Bringing back the federal energy programs that ended

These programs paid for efficiency and all-electric work in affordable housing. The 2025 tax law ended the credits and rescinded the retrofit money, so the gap they filled now lands on soft lenders.

Why 45L worked as gap money for tax credit deals. The statute says the usual basis cut for the credit "shall not apply for purposes of determining the adjusted basis of any building under section 42" (26 U.S.C. 45L(e)). A tax credit building could take 45L without losing any housing credit equity. On a 60-home building that met Zero Energy Ready with prevailing wages, the statute's numbers come to $300,000 (60 × $5,000). Illustrative arithmetic, not any one deal.

What reinstatement could look like, cheapest first

  1. Restore 45L for income-restricted homes only. Limit it to homes in a building with a section 42, HOME, HTF or USDA restriction, keep the section 42 basis carve-out, and keep the prevailing-wage tier. Full repeal saved $5.4B over ten years, so an affordable-only version would cost less. JCT has not scored that version; treat its cost as unknown.
  2. Restore 179D for multifamily rental buildings that are income-restricted. Full repeal saved only $134M, so this is the cheapest item on the list.
  3. Refund GRRP or fold it into HOME. HUD could instead release the HOME green-building subsidy increase it put on hold in April 2026, which costs no new money (section 0).
  4. Keep the 48E low-income solar bonus allocations running while solar projects can still qualify.

Why it matters for climate goals: these programs reached the households least able to pay for electrification, in buildings that stay rent-restricted for decades. No Colorado emissions estimate for them was found, so this page makes no emissions claim.

What this means for the federal ask. States carry building electrification now, because the federal tools for new affordable housing ended in 2026. 45L ends for homes acquired after 30 June 2026, 179D ends for construction starting after that date, and HUD's Green and Resilient Retrofit Program money was rescinded (P.L. 119-21; Barclay Damon, 10 July 2025, secondary). Three low-cost federal moves remain (section 3C has the full reinstatement options):

  1. HUD can take the HOME green-building subsidy increase off indefinite hold. That is an admin action.
  2. Treasury can keep the 48E low-income solar bonus allocations running for as long as solar projects can still qualify under the 2025 tax law's deadlines. Not confirmed: whether a 2027 allocation round will open.
  3. Congress can add a small LIHTC basis boost for all-electric or certified low-energy buildings. Because the cost lands in the tax credit, it reaches the units that need it without a new program.

What a federal program would need

  1. Steady PRICE money. The program is authorized through 2033 but unfunded for 2026 and proposed for elimination in 2027. A multi-year set amount would let resident groups line up purchases, which move on a seller's timeline.
  2. A federal acquisition guarantee for resident and nonprofit buyers. Colorado's fund shows the model: a state revolving fund run through CDFIs. A federal guarantee, through the CDFI Fund or USDA, would let those lenders go further. Proposal; we did not check whether existing USDA or CDFI Bond Guarantee rules already allow park acquisition.
  3. Tie federal park financing to tenant protections. Duty to Serve already scores "MHCs with certain pad lease protections." FHFA could require those protections, plus a resident notice and opportunity-to-purchase clause like Colorado's, on every park loan Fannie, Freddie or FHA backs.
  4. Bigger Duty to Serve targets. Two or three resident-owned park loans a year from Fannie is not a national program.
  5. Home financing on the lot. Chattel lending is still a pilot discussion. The ROAD Act chassis and Title I changes help new homes, but most park homes are financed as personal property.

5B. A federal shared equity program for renters and owners

The "first position" problem. A first-mortgage lender wants to be able to sell a foreclosed home at full market price. So federal lending rules make the affordability terms give way:

  • FHA: price and resale limits are allowed only if "the restrictions will automatically terminate if title to the mortgaged property is transferred by foreclosure or deed-in-lieu of foreclosure, or if the mortgage is assigned to the Secretary" (24 CFR 203.41(c)(2)).
  • Fannie Mae: land trust ground lease limits "must terminate automatically upon foreclosure" (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B5-5.3-02, 5 Feb 2025).
  • HUD HOME: affordability limits "may terminate upon ... foreclosure, transfer in lieu of foreclosure, or assignment of an FHA-insured mortgage to HUD." A city can instead use "purchase options, rights of first refusal, or other preemptive rights to obtain ownership of the housing before foreclosure" (24 CFR 92.254).

So each home that goes to foreclosure can leave the program, and the public money in it is lost. Mt. Crested Butte lost "several of its 'affordable' units to foreclosure," because "banks and lenders typically require that the town lift any restrictions on foreclosed properties" (Crested Butte News, 12 Jun 2013).

What a federal program could do for owners.

  1. Let limits survive when a steward stands behind them. Amend 24 CFR 203.41(c)(2), and have FHFA direct Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, so that resale limits may survive foreclosure when a qualified steward (a land trust, housing authority or state housing agency) holds a recorded right to buy the home at the restricted price before a foreclosure sale. HOME already lets cities use that right.

  2. Make the first lender whole instead of wiping out the limits. A small federal guarantee, run through FHA or a HOME set-aside, would pay the lender the gap between the restricted price and the loan balance if the steward buys. The lender keeps first claim on the money, but the affordability terms stay on the home. That takes away the reason lenders need the limits wiped out. What a federal program could do for renters.

  1. Open HUD's savings accounts to more renters. HUD's Family Self-Sufficiency program already builds tenant savings: the owner deposits an escrow credit "each month ... to each family's FSS escrow account" as the family's earnings rise (24 CFR 984.305). It covers public housing, vouchers and HUD-assisted multifamily (§984.101), not tax credit or USDA 538 tenants who have no HUD rent help. Congress could let state housing agencies run it in those buildings.
  2. Fund renter equity in federally backed buildings. Allow a share of cash flow or sale gains in tax credit and 538 properties to go into tenant accounts, as Colorado Renter Rewards does in Prop 123 buildings (2% cash back plus a 2% savings match; Governor's Office, 5 Feb 2026; ImpactAlpha, 16 Mar 2026), and count that payment as an allowed project expense.
  3. Keep payouts from costing families their benefits. Colorado's program lets renters defer payouts "to avoid affecting public assistance" (ImpactAlpha, 16 Mar 2026). A federal rule that excludes renter-equity payouts from income for HUD rent, SNAP and Medicaid would remove that trap. Not confirmed: exactly how each benefit program counts these payouts today.

What this means in Colorado

  • Colorado's 2026 code does not require all-electric construction, and CHFA gives no points for it. So any federal electrification incentive reaches Colorado deals through cost (rebates, credits, lower utility allowances), not through scoring.
  • The Fort Collins BABA waiver is the clearest local example of federal overhead on a small affordable rehab.
  • Colorado park buyouts already run on state money (the SB22-160 fund) and local money (Roaring Fork). A federal program would add to that, not start it.
  • The 25% bond test and the extra 12% of 9% credit are already law and apply to Colorado's 2026 credit year.
