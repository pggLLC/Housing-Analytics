Services in Affordable Housing

Resident services, supportive housing and service agreements

An affordable apartment solves the rent. It does not, on its own, solve a lost job, a missed medication, a hospital stay or a notice on the door. Services are the help that comes with the home: someone on site or on call who connects residents to what they need before a problem becomes an eviction. This page explains what those services are, how they help, how strong the evidence is, what Colorado requires, and who pays.

The short version

  • Two different things share the word "services". Resident services are light-touch help that many affordable properties offer all their residents. Supportive housing pairs a home with intensive, voluntary case management for people leaving homelessness or living with a disability.
  • The strongest evidence is about staying housed, and some of it is from Denver. In Denver's supportive housing social impact bond trial, 77 percent of participants still living were stably housed three years after moving in (Urban Institute, 2021). Evidence that it improves health or saves public money overall is thinner, and this page says so where it applies.
  • Services pay back partly to the public, but not in full. Denver participants had 40 percent fewer arrests, 27 percent fewer jail days and 65 percent fewer detox visits. Those avoided costs covered about half of the program's cost; the rest was a net public cost.
  • CHFA rewards services, and Colorado now has a Medicaid route to pay for them. CHFA gives 15 points for setting aside at least 25 percent of units for people experiencing homelessness or another special population, with services. Since July 1, 2025, Health First Colorado (Medicaid) can pay enrolled providers for tenancy services, and DOH's 2026 supportive housing round gives full points only to applicants that include Medicaid.
  • Funding is still the weak link. Buildings are financed for 30 years or more; services money is usually renewed a year at a time. DOH treats $7,200 per household per year as the minimum for good supportive housing services. A services plan without a durable source of that money is a promise, not a plan.

Two kinds of services

Most affordable properties offer some services; a smaller number are built around them. The difference matters for who lives there, how much it costs, and what results you can expect.

Resident servicesPermanent supportive housing
Who it servesEvery household in an ordinary affordable property: families, older adults, working households.People leaving long-term homelessness, or living with a serious mental illness, substance use disorder, intellectual or developmental disability, or other disability, often in a set-aside of units within a larger property.
What it looks likeA resident services coordinator who knows residents by name, connects them to benefits, food, health care, child care, jobs and financial coaching, and steps in early when rent falls behind.A case manager with a small caseload, help getting and keeping benefits, links to behavioral health and medical care, tenancy support (how to be a tenant, how to resolve a dispute), and crisis response.
Is it required of residents?No. Residents choose what to use.No. Quality supportive housing is voluntary: keeping the apartment does not depend on taking part in treatment. This is the core of the Housing First approach.

Between the two sit properties for older adults, where services help residents age in place (wellness checks, transportation, help with Medicaid and Medicare), and properties for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, where a disability services agency provides support while residents hold their own leases.

How services help

Services work by catching problems early, while they are still small. Read the chain left to right (top to bottom on a phone). The research in the next section tests parts of this chain, and the evidence is not equally strong for every link: it is strongest for staying housed and for jail, shelter and detox use, and weaker for health, income and property finances.

  1. 1. The service

    Someone notices and connects

    • A coordinator or case manager who residents already know
    • Help applying for benefits, food assistance, Medicaid
    • Links to health, behavioral health and disability care
    • Financial coaching, rent payment plans, job help
  2. 2. For the resident

    A crisis is caught early

    • Late rent becomes a payment plan, not a filing
    • Income rises when benefits are claimed
    • Health problems are treated before an emergency
    • The household stays in the home
  3. 3. For the property

    Operations steady

    • Fewer evictions and the legal costs that come with them
    • Fewer move-outs, so less turnover and vacancy
    • Less bad debt and fewer damaged units
    • A stronger case at CHFA and with investors
  4. 4. For the public

    Costly systems are used less

    • Fewer nights in jail and fewer arrests
    • Fewer detox stays and emergency room visits
    • Fewer shelter nights

Why the savings do not pay the bill. The property keeps some of the gain (less turnover and bad debt), but most of it lands in other budgets: the jail, the hospital, the shelter, Medicaid. That is why services are usually paid for by those public systems, by grants, or by a reserve set aside at construction, and rarely by rents alone. It is also why services money is the first thing to disappear when a grant ends.

What the research does not show

Services are sometimes oversold. These are the claims to be careful with.

  • "Supportive housing pays for itself." Not in Denver's own trial. Even for people chosen because they used the most jail, detox and emergency care, avoided costs offset about half of the program cost, $6,876 per person per year; the other half was a net public cost. Savings depend on who is housed, and a project serving a less costly population should expect smaller offsets.
  • "Supportive housing improves health." Staying housed is well established. Measured improvements in health, substance use and mental health are weaker and less consistent. The National Academies review (card above) found too little evidence to say supportive housing improves health outcomes, while noting it is hard to study.
  • "Resident services cut evictions by X percent." Most resident services studies compare properties that chose to offer services with those that did not, so part of the difference may come from better management. Treat single percentages as suggestive, not as a guarantee for your property.
  • "Any services will do." Results come from specific models: voluntary services, small caseloads, staff trained in tenancy support, and a real connection to health and behavioral health care. Denver's follow-on Housing to Health program runs one staff member for every 12 participants; DOH's benchmark is one case manager for every 15 households. A community room and a flyer board are not a services program.

Supportive service agreements

When an owner does not provide services itself, it signs an agreement with a service provider, usually called a memorandum of understanding (MOU) or services agreement. CHFA requires executed, dated MOUs for its supportive housing points (see the next section). A workable agreement settles these points in writing before the building opens:

  1. Who is served and how they are referred. The target population, how many units, and the referral source, which in Colorado is often a Continuum of Care's coordinated entry system or a housing voucher program.
  2. What services are offered. Case management, tenancy support, benefits help, behavioral health and medical links, crisis response, and what is on site versus by referral.
  3. Staffing. How many staff, with what caseload per worker, what hours, and what happens when a position is vacant.
  4. Money and its term. The annual services budget, who pays each part, how long each source is committed, and what happens when a source ends. This is the clause most often left vague.
  5. Roles of the property manager and the provider. Property management enforces the lease; the provider supports the tenant. The agreement says how they talk to each other before a lease violation becomes an eviction, without making services a condition of tenancy.
  6. Space. Private offices for confidential meetings, a community room, and who pays for them.
  7. Privacy and data. What resident information is shared, with consent, and which outcomes are reported (housing retention, move-outs, evictions, income).
  8. Review and exit. Regular meetings, how disputes are resolved, notice before either side leaves, and how a replacement provider is found.

What Colorado's tax credit plan requires

CHFA's Qualified Allocation Plan (QAP) decides who wins Colorado's housing tax credits. It rewards services in three places: points for setting aside units for people experiencing homelessness or other special populations, points for deep income targeting, and a larger developer fee that must be spent on services or rent help. Each card quotes the plan, and the quotes are re-checked against CHFA's published plan text.

Who pays for services

Capital funding (tax credits, loans, grants) pays to build the home. Services need their own money, every year, for as long as residents need them. These are the main sources in Colorado. Each carries the date it was last checked and is flagged here once it is due for a re-check.

What services cost

Services costs are reported per household or per unit per year. They vary with the population, the caseload and local wages, so use these as a starting range and price your own plan with your provider.

Questions for a project team

Settle these before the application, not after the award. They are the questions CHFA, investors and service providers will ask.

  • Who will live here, and what will they need? A family property, an older-adult property and a supportive housing set-aside need different services and different budgets. Your Housing Needs Assessment shows how many households in the area pay more than half their income in rent and how many are extremely low income.
  • Who provides the services, and have they done it before? CHFA requires providers with at least three years of experience for its supportive housing points.
  • What does it cost each year, and who pays for each year? Write down every source and when it ends. A plan funded for three years in a building financed for thirty needs a stated answer for year four.
  • Is there rent help for the residents you plan to serve? Households with very low incomes usually need a voucher or operating subsidy as well as services. Without one, the rent is still out of reach.
  • Is there room for it? Offices with doors, a community room, and space close to where residents already walk.
  • How will you know it is working? Track housing retention, evictions, move-outs and turnover from the start, so the results can support the next round of funding.
