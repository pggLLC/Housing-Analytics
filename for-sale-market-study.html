For-Sale Market Study screening

Use this page to screen how a proposed for-sale project might serve local buyers, perform under different land and resale structures, and sell over time. It cannot confirm market demand or project feasibility. Supply the project-specific assumptions and evidence you have; anything you do not supply is marked as unavailable instead of being guessed.

New to this? Start here

The question this page answers: if your community built a small group of homes for sale at below-market prices, could local working households afford them, would enough of them actually buy, and what happens to the price when an owner later sells?

You do not need to fill anything in to learn from it. Read the sections in order:

  • Local sale prices — what homes around you have recently sold for.
  • 1. The project — how many homes, and the most each income group could pay compared with typical local home values.
  • 2. The land — four ways to handle the land under the homes, and what each one trades off.
  • 3. Reselling — how a resale formula tries to keep the home affordable for the next buyer, whether it succeeds, and what the seller walks away with.
  • 4. Where the money goes — one sale, line by line.
  • 5. Who could buy — local households narrowed down to likely buyers. This step needs local evidence you supply.
  • 6. How fast they might sell — only works once section 5 is filled in.
  • 7. Report — the same screen as a formal draft you can download, once sections 5 and 6 can be screened for your area.

"Owner input required" is not an error. It marks a number that has to come from the project's sponsor, or from local evidence you enter in section 5; the page leaves it blank rather than guess.

What the terms on this page mean
AMI (Area Median Income)
The middle income for the area, set each year by HUD. "80% of AMI" means a household earning 80% of that middle income.
Income group (AMI band)
A range of incomes, such as 80–90% of AMI. Each home in the project is priced for one group.
Most they could pay
The highest home price a household in the middle of that group could afford, counting the mortgage payment, property taxes, homeowner's insurance and mortgage insurance.
Gap
How far the typical local home value is above what that group can pay. Down-payment help or public funds would have to cover it.
Resale formula (shared equity)
A rule written into the deed or lease that caps what an owner can sell for, aiming to keep the home affordable. Whether it does depends on how prices and incomes move. The owner still builds some wealth, but less than on the open market.
Ground lease / CLT
The buyer owns the house but leases the land, usually from a community land trust (CLT) or the town, for a small monthly fee. Leaving the land out of the price is what makes the home cheaper.
Deed restriction
The buyer owns house and land outright, but a legal restriction on the deed limits who can buy it next and for how much.
Fee simple / leasehold
Fee simple is ordinary ownership of the house and the land under it. Leasehold means owning the house while leasing the land, as in a ground lease.
Steward
The organization that checks buyers qualify and enforces the resale formula over time.
Net proceeds
What the seller walks away with after the mortgage, selling costs and any public help are repaid.
Recapture
Public money paid back to the town or agency when a home is sold.
Buyer funnel (effective demand)
Start from a rough count of local moderate-income renter households, then narrow it by shares you supply for who could and would buy. The result is an estimate that depends on those shares, not a count of committed buyers.
Capture
The share of that estimated buyer pool the project would need to sell all its homes. A very high share is a warning sign.
Scenario, not a prediction
A "what if" run — for example, "if home values and incomes both rise 3% a year". It shows how the math behaves, not what will happen.
Calculated estimate
A number this page worked out from stated assumptions, not one taken from a published source.

7. Report (screening draft)

In plain terms: the same screen written up as a document you can save or share with the professionals who would check it — lenders, appraisers, attorneys and market analysts. Each section opens with a plain-language summary, and the report ends with a list of the terms it uses.

Standing disclosure: This is a screening estimate, not a completed market study. Verification parties include the project owner, broker, lender, appraiser, assessor, attorney, steward, administrator, and a professional market analyst. Methods are exposed through classification pills, evidence-basis text, engine denominators, policy-source links, and caveats; no displayed scenario is a prediction.
