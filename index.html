What brings you here?

Across Colorado, roughly half of renters now spend more than 30% of every paycheck on rent (ACS B25070) — and counting homeowners too, nearly 1 in 3 Colorado households, about 694,000, carry that same cost burden (HUD CHAS 2018–2022). COHO is built for people who develop, plan, finance and govern housing — developers, planners, housing authorities, local governments and lenders — working from the same public sources (ACS, HUD CHAS, CHFA and DOLA) so everyone looks at the same picture of where new homes will fit. If you are learning the field, you are welcome too: each step explains its terms as you go. Educational; not a substitute for lender underwriting or legal advice.

From public data to a housing decision

Whatever path a project takes — tax-credit rental, deed-restricted or shared-equity ownership, preserving homes that are already affordable — the questions come in the same order: where, who needs what, does the market support it, does the deal pencil, and what do you recommend. Paths to affordable housing — and how they differ

There is more than one conventional way to get an affordable home built or kept. The tools above ask the same questions of each; what changes is the capital, the timeline, and the obligations that follow closing.

Tax-credit rental (LIHTC). The largest federal source for affordable rental homes: investors buy dollar-for-dollar federal credits, and the homes stay restricted to households at 30–80% of Area Median Income. In Colorado, CHFA awards the credits competitively under its Qualified Allocation Plan; most applicants do not win on the first try, and a project typically takes 3–7 years from site to move-in. The conventions to know: the QAP scorecard, a 15-year compliance period that usually runs 30 or more, and equity pricing that moves with the market.

Affordable ownership. Deed-restricted for-sale homes, community land trusts, and shared-equity models keep a home affordable at resale rather than at rent. The capital is different — down-payment assistance, soft second mortgages, land held by a trust — and so are the risks: the resale formula, stewardship of the restriction over decades, and whether buyers can get financing.

Preservation. Buying and repairing homes that are already affordable — tax-credit properties nearing the end of their compliance period, older buildings that are affordable by default — is often faster and cheaper than building. The risks sit in acquisition timing, rehab scope, and keeping rents affordable through the transition.

Public and partnership routes. Housing authorities, employers, and Colorado's Prop 123 land banking can contribute land or capital without a tax credit at all. The questions are the same; the capital stack is what changes.

Affordable housing is not just a real estate transaction.

It is a public health intervention. Research consistently links stable, affordable housing to lower rates of emergency room utilization, improved school attendance, reduced child welfare involvement, and greater long-term economic security. The evidence is clear and has been for decades.

It is also a question of community. The working family putting nearly half of every paycheck toward rent, the senior on a fixed income, the neighbor finding their way out of homelessness — these are people you know. They need homes that fit their communities: architecturally, socially, and financially. Not warehouses. Homes.

This reference exists to make the data plainer for everyone who has a hand in building those homes. So a developer can see the same picture a planner does. So an elected official has the words to advocate honestly. So a financier and a community member can read the same sources and reach the same conclusions.
