How this site was built and checked

pggLLC built COHO Analytics with AI-assisted coding and analysis. AI does not decide what is true here. Every measured figure comes from a named public source (such as the Census Bureau, HUD, CHFA, DOLA, BLS or FRED), and the page that shows it names that source. Where the site estimates instead of measuring, such as projections or the commuter and replacement-loss rates in §09 below, the page says so and states the assumption. On every change, automated tests check many published figures against the source files they come from. Hand-kept reference data, such as policy rules and pricing factors, carries a review-by date, and the checks flag a file once that date passes so it is re-read against its source. 01 Notation and conventions

Throughout, P is a place (municipality or census-designated place), T a census tract, b an AMI band, and t a tenure in {renter, owner}. CHAS bands are the five the source publishes: ≤30, 31–50, 51–80, 81–100, >100 percent of area median income.

One convention governs everything else and is stated first because it changes what the other formulas mean: a quantity that cannot be measured is null, and null is never coerced to zero. In the formulas below, any sum or quotient with a null operand is null, not a value computed as though the operand were zero. §07 gives the propagation rules precisely, because they are where most of the system's defects have lived.

02 Apportionment: tract to place

HUD's CHAS tables are the only public source crossing cost burden with AMI band and tenure. They publish at tract level. Municipal boundaries do not follow tracts, so every place-level figure in this system is an apportionment, and the weight is the whole argument.

The file's own header documents a different rule

build_place_chas.py opened by describing “area-weighted apportionment (share_of_tract_area)” long after F28 replaced that rule, and its output-schema example carried the same superseded string. The executing code takes the maximum of area share and population share, clamped to 1.0, falling back to area share alone where population is unavailable — and flagging those places. Both the header and the example are now corrected, and test/place-chas-method-honesty.test.js fails if they drift again.

A correction to an earlier version of this section. It said the published metadata was wrong today. That overstated it. The method string actually emitted into data/hna/place-chas.json was never the flatly-wrong area-weighted claim — that string lived in a docstring example. The emitted string said “Population-share apportionment: weight = min(1, population_share),” which is incomplete rather than wrong: it omits the max(area_share, …), so for a tract lying mostly inside a place it names the wrong term as the winner. The emitter now states the full rule, and since the CHAS build was re-run the published file carries it too.

This matters beyond tidiness. max is not a partition: summed over the tracts overlapping a place it does not equal 1, and summed over the places overlapping a tract it can exceed 1. Households can therefore be counted into more than one place. §03 is what keeps that from reaching the published levels, which makes the anchor load-bearing rather than cosmetic.

The assumption underneath both shares is uniform distribution of households within a tract. It is poorest for slivers — tracts contributing under roughly a tenth of their area — which are flagged rather than silently included, and it is exactly where max does the most work.

03 The ACS occupied-household anchor

Apportionment is geometrically defensible and demographically optimistic. Summed across overlapping tracts it overcounts households in 149 of the 406 Colorado places the anchor can be checked against in the current build (37%: the apportioned household total exceeds the ACS occupied total before anchoring), and under the max rule it must. The other 76 of 482 places lack ACS tenure counts, so whether they overcount is not known. The correction anchors each place's tenure totals to an independent source — the American Community Survey's occupied-unit counts — and scales the bands to fit.

Tenure-wise level anchor λ_t = A_t / Σ_b M(P, b, t) M*(P, b, t) = λ_t · M(P, b, t)
A_renter
ACS DP04_0047E, renter-occupied units in P
A_owner
ACS DP04_0046E, owner-occupied units in P
λ_t

The property that makes this defensible

Because λ_t is constant across bands within a tenure, every rate is invariant under the anchor and only levels change. A cost-burden share — the quantity a policy is actually written against — survives the correction unchanged, while the household count becomes checkable against a source that did not produce it. Formally, for any bands b, b' of the same tenure, M*(b)/M*(b') = M(b)/M(b').

The ordering is load-bearing and is a live operational hazard: the ACS summary caches must regenerate before the CHAS build, or λ_t is computed against stale denominators and the anchor silently no-ops — producing a file that looks normal and carries uncorrected levels.

04 Cost burden and published shares

Cost burden follows HUD's definition: a household is burdened above 30% of gross income for housing, severely burdened above 50%. Both are counted directly in CHAS rather than derived, so the apportionment above carries them without further assumption.

Published counts, then published shares C_t = round( Σ_b M*(P, b, t), 1 ) B30_t = round( Σ_b M*30(P, b, t), 1 ) share30_t = round( B30_t / C_t , 4 ) ← from the PUBLISHED counts scripts/hna/build_place_chas.py — recompute_summary()
Why the share divides published counts rather than raw accumulators

Two reasons, and the second is the interesting one. Consistency: a reader dividing the published burdened count by the published total now obtains the published share. Before this change they did not — one place shipped counts of 6.9 and 16 alongside a share of 0.4312, where 6.9/16 is 0.4313.

Reproducibility: the raw accumulators are sums of floats that land on values like 16.000000000000004. Several shares sit on an exact .00005 rounding tie — 9.3/48, 104.7/240, 177.5/400, 6.9/16 — where a one-ulp difference in the denominator flips the fourth decimal. The file then differed between machines, and a freshness checker reported places as stale whose inputs had not changed in months. Rounding the denominator first makes the division consume identical IEEE doubles on every run.

Rounding is not distributive, and the page says so

Counts are rounded to one decimal before summing and to whole households for display. Column totals are therefore computed from rounded values, so a displayed subtotal can differ by a household or two from the sum of unrounded bands. The published tables are internally consistent — the columns add up — at the cost of being a rounded view rather than an exact one. An earlier draft of the working paper reported a renter subtotal of 227 where its own column summed to 228; the generated version now takes the subtotal from the rounded bands, so it agrees with what a reader can add.

05 Ownership affordability — seven models, not one

Read this before citing any affordability figure from this system

There is no single “affordability model” here. The registry defines 7 models with back and front ratio conventions between them, and they answer different questions. The default is conservative_screening.

The working paper's methodology section previously described this as a single model solving at “43% back-end DTI, 20% down, 6.5%, 0.65% tax, 0.85% insurance.” That is a recognisable description of conventional_dti — one of the seven, and not the default. The default screens at a 30% front-end ratio, which is a materially smaller number. That section has been corrected; it is recorded here because a reader who cited the old description would have cited a model the system does not run by default.

The structure is a closed-form solve rather than a search: every cost that scales with price is collected into k, the cost per dollar of price per month, and the budget is divided by it. Fixed monthly costs are subtracted from the budget first, precisely because they do not scale. There are two inverses — income required for a given price — and both are in use. incomeRequiredForPrice() runs the same algebra backwards in closed form; the affordability metrics panel and the HNA utilities use it. incomeNeededForHomeValue() is a binary search, because f(size) and the PMI gate make the forward function piecewise; the ownership strategy and the section takeaways use it, and it returns null when no income up to $8,000,000 reaches the price (the search doubles from $250,000 and stops at its first step past $5,000,000).

The distinction is substantive and is the reason the default is not conventional_dti. HUD calls a household cost burdened above 30% of income for housing. A model solving at a 43% back-end ratio answers “what will an underwriter approve,” which is a strictly larger number than “what can this household carry without being cost burdened.” Both are legitimate questions. A subsidy sized to the first delivers a buyer who is cost burdened on the day they move in — and a jurisdiction that reported need using the 30% screen and then sized assistance using the 43% one would be internally inconsistent in a way no single published figure would reveal.

06 The scoring chain

The ranking index reduces every jurisdiction to one number. This section writes out the whole chain, because a composite score is the easiest place in a system like this to hide an arbitrary choice, and the only defence is publishing every weight.

Two properties are worth stating before the formulas. First, almost every input enters as a percentile rank within its own geography type, not as a raw value — counties are ranked against counties, places against places. Second, the percentile convention is rank / (n − 1) · 100, so the lowest-ranked geography in each pool scores exactly 0 and the highest exactly 100 by construction. Scores are therefore positional, not absolute: a place cannot improve its score by improving, only by improving relative to its peers.

Need is also read from the jobs a place hosts

The resident gap stops at the municipal boundary, so a place that has priced its workforce out reads as satisfied: the households it lost are no longer residents. The workforce reading puts them back. It adds the households of the people who commute in, at or below 60% AMI, to the renter households at or below 60% AMI who live here, and sets both against rental units affordable at ≤60% AMI, not the ≤30% band the resident gap uses. The in-commuters are LODES workplace counts turned into households by Census microdata (§09), so two workers in one household are one home and a job’s pay is read as its household’s income. Until October 2026 the reading set one low-wage job against one unit, and counted the jobs of residents who were already on the demand side.

The two readings meet through max, never a blend, so adding the workforce reading can raise a gap score and never lower one. In the current build it is the higher of the two for 240 of 546 geographies. It does not set the 20-year figure, because it counts every in-commuter and is a shortfall today (§09). That figure adds today’s in-commuters who would move closer to projected resident growth instead, for 536 geographies, and future_units_reading says which parts each row carries.

The augment can only ever raise a score

commuter is a percentile in [0, 100], so the multiplier lies in [1, 1 + α] and the term is strictly non-negative. A jurisdiction with no in-commuting is not penalised; one with heavy in-commuting is credited by at most 20%. This is a deliberate asymmetry: in-commuting is evidence that a place's workforce cannot live there, which is additional need — but its absence is not evidence of the reverse, because a place with no jobs also has no in-commuters.

The min(100, ·) is a genuine saturation: a jurisdiction already at the top of its pool receives no augment at all, so the term compresses precisely where it would matter most for ranking.

The confidence multiplier deducts 0.03 per imputed score factor and 0.01 per approximated field, with further deductions for low-confidence home values and for opportunity data resolved only at county level. It is floored at 0.85, so the maximum total penalty is 15%.

Three properties a user of this score should know

Need is double-percentiled. The five factors enter need_core as percentiles; need_aug is then itself converted to a percentile before the axis blend. Ranking a set of ranks compresses the distribution twice and makes the final score's spacing arbitrary — differences of a point or two carry no interpretation.

Opportunity is not percentiled at that stage. It enters the axis blend as a raw 0–100 composite while need enters as a percentile. The two axes are therefore on different scales despite being weighted as though comparable.

The confidence multiplier scales, it does not flag. A jurisdiction whose inputs are largely imputed loses at most 15% of its score and still ranks. The multiplier is published alongside the score, and a user comparing two jurisdictions should read it, because a high score at a low multiplier and a slightly lower score at full confidence are not the same claim.

07 Absence propagation

The rules below are the ones that make the rest of this document mean what it says. They exist because Number(null) is 0 and 0 is finite, so a coercion upstream of any formula here produces a value that passes every downstream validity check.

Renormalisation over present factors Σ_f∈F* W_f · f weighted_avg(F) = ─────────────── F* = { f ∈ F : f is a number } Σ_f∈F* W_f = null if F* is empty scripts/hna/build_ranking_index.py — _weighted_average()

A missing factor is dropped and the remaining weights are renormalised, rather than the factor entering as zero. The difference is not subtle: as a zero, a missing overcrowding rate would push a jurisdiction's need score down by a tenth of its value and look like measured absence of overcrowding. Renormalised, it means “this score was computed from the other four factors” — which is a weaker claim, correctly stated.

The other rules, in the order they bite:

  • Rate denominators have a floor. A rate computed on fewer than 50 households is not published. Small denominators produce rates of 0% and 100% that are arithmetically correct and substantively meaningless.
  • Sentinels are rejected before arithmetic. The Census API returns -666666666 for unavailable variables. It is finite, so every naive check passes it through; it is caught at parse.
  • Guard ≤ 0, not < 0. Wherever zero is not a meaningful value — a price, a rent, an income — exactly zero means unknown rather than free.
  • Negative inputs yield null, not 0. The affordability solve returns null for missing or negative income, and 0 only for the distinct case where fixed monthly costs consume the entire budget — a real finding, not a missing one.
  • Reasons travel with values. Every metric carries source, vintage, geography level and confidence, so a consumer can distinguish “not published by the source” from “below the reporting threshold” from “measured.”

08 Reproducibility and known defects

8.1 · What makes a run reproducible

Share denominators are rounded before division (§04), which removes the float-tie instability. Generated files are rebuilt in continuous integration and a pull request whose outputs drift from what its inputs would produce fails. The constants in this document are extracted from source rather than transcribed, and the counts it quotes about the data are read from the build that produced them. The formulas are written by hand, so they are held to the code by fingerprint instead: a change to any function a section names fails the build until someone has re-read that section against it. That catches a change of shape, which no constant check can — the workforce reading in §06 arrived without moving a single weight, and this page described the score without it for three weeks.

8.2 · Defects this document found

Writing out the specification located four disagreements between code and documentation:

  1. The apportionment weight is min(1.0, max(area, population)) while the file header and its output-schema example both said area-weighted (§02). Both corrected and now guarded. The string actually emitted into the data file was incomplete rather than wrong — it omitted the max — and is also corrected, in the published file as well.
  2. The working paper described one affordability model where 7 exist, and described the non-default one (§05).
  3. Affordability defaults are declared in six places, not two. Down payment takes three values — one of them a whole-number percentage rather than a fraction — and insurance is declared in two different units: flat annual dollars in one file, a rate of home value in another. On a $250,000 home those differ by roughly 2.7×, crossing near $686,000. test/affordability-defaults-inventory.test.js now pins all of them and fails when a new competing default appears (§05).
  4. A tenure label in the working paper's tractability table inverted its two zero-cases, publishing a jurisdiction with 266 burdened renters and no burdened owners as “owner only.” Fixed, and now guarded against the counts rather than against the function that produced them.

8.3 · Open methodological weaknesses

  • The max weight is not a partition. It can attribute a household to more than one place. The ACS anchor bounds the consequence at the place level but nothing bounds it statewide, so summing place-level counts across Colorado is not a valid operation and the interface should say so more loudly than it does.
  • An exported workforce is counted more than once. The same workers raise the gap factor through the workforce reading and the future-pressure factor through the in-commuters in the 20-year figure, and in-commuting raises need again through the commuter augment. Each is defensible alone; together they weight one phenomenon more heavily than the factor weights in Table 3 suggest (§06).
  • Double percentiling compresses the need axis and puts it on a different scale from opportunity in the same weighted blend (§06).
  • Uniform within-tract density is assumed by both shares and is weakest exactly where max is doing the most work.
  • Cost burden and unit gap use different income bases — burden against area medians, gap against household-size-adjusted limits. Both are correct against their own definitions and they are not comparable band-for-band, which is a trap for any reader who assumes a shared denominator.
  • The 30%/43% divide is unresolved by design and should be resolved by the user, not by the system picking one.
What would falsify any of this

Each claim here is checkable against the repository. The weights are in scripts/hna/build_ranking_index.py; the apportionment and anchor in scripts/hna/build_place_chas.py; the affordability solve in js/hna/ownership-finance.js against data/policy/affordability-models.json. A reviewer who finds this page disagreeing with any of them has found a real defect, and the extraction script named at the top is where to look first.

09 What future need counts, and what it leaves out

The 20-year “homes needed” figure answers one question: how many homes must be added for the households projected here and for the people who already work here but live elsewhere. It does not answer how many homes this place will lose. Professional needs assessments usually add that second number. The Pueblo Housing Assessment and Strategy (Gruen Gruen + Associates, September 2021) builds its ten-year county need of 9,561 homes from workforce demand of 4,325, senior demand of 3,649 and replacement of 1,587 homes lost from the existing stock. This section states which of those parts the figure here contains.

Two parts, added

The first part is resident growth: the rise in homes the projected households need, at the target vacancy, from the base year to the horizon. No count of today’s homes enters it. Subtracting today’s stock told resort counties they had surplus, because the census counts second homes and short-term rentals as vacant. The HNA page and the ranking compute it the same way. The page’s Hold and Trend headship options read projected population × a headship rate in place of the household forecast, and the figure’s name says so.

The second part is the in-commuter need, the homes for people who work here and cannot live here. It takes the people who commute in (LEHD LODES origin–destination, 2023) in LODES’s three pay tiers, counting each worker’s primary job only so that a person with two jobs is one person. Census microdata (ACS PUMS 2020–2024) turns each tier into households. Each worker in the tier, in the county’s place-of-work area, counts as one household divided by the wage earners in it, and the household’s income is set against CHFA’s income limits for the county and the household’s size, brought to the limits’ year by CPI as HUD does. A tier’s households per job at or below 120% AMI, times its in-commuters, gives the homes. Households above 120% AMI are left out. That is the top of CHFA’s regular income tiers; Prop 123 reaches 160% in twelve rural resort counties, and the figure stays at 120% there too. This is the chain a jobs-housing nexus study uses: jobs, then worker households, then income tiers (Keyser Marston Associates, 2013). The figure then takes the share who would move closer if they could. People who work in Colorado but live in another state are only in the LODES auxiliary file, so they are added from it; without them the counties on the New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming lines read low. The microdata are a sample, so the HNA page prints a 90% margin from the survey’s replicate weights beside the figure; it is under a tenth of the figure wherever the figure is ten homes or more. No statewide source measures the share who would move. Local employer surveys do, so the figure counts a quarter and the HNA page also shows a half. Summed over the 64 counties it is 132,511 to 265,019 homes. For Colorado as a whole the HNA page counts only the in-commuters who live in another state, because a worker who moves between Colorado counties leaves a Colorado home behind. It is a deficit today, not growth, but it is added because a household-growth projection cannot see people who are not residents. Where a geography has no in-commuter record, the figure is resident growth alone and says so. Where projected households fall by more than the in-commuter need, the figure is below zero, and the site reads it as a surplus of that many homes, never as a negative need.

Until October 2026 the figure was instead the larger of resident growth and a jobs reading: the lower-wage jobs located in the place against rental homes affordable at ≤60% AMI, one job to one home. That reading counted jobs, not households, included jobs held by residents who already have a home, and put a shortfall that exists today in a 20-year slot. The ranking’s workforce gap score (§06) now reads that shortfall in households: renters here and the households of everyone who commutes in, at or below 60% AMI, against rentals affordable at ≤60% AMI. Summed over the counties it is 186,093 homes. It counts every in-commuter, not a share who would move, so it is not added to the figure; the HNA page shows it on its own line.

No line converts forecast job growth into homes. The State Demography Office’s county forecasts combine a labour-supply model with its own jobs forecast to set migration (State Demography Office, 2018), so the resident-growth reading already houses the workers that forecast expects. Adding the jobs forecast on top would count the same workers twice.

The rates follow the pattern in HUD’s Components of Inventory Change reports, where older homes leave the stock faster. They are set so that Pueblo County lands inside the Pueblo assessment’s range. They are a calibration, not an estimate measured for each county. Over 20 years the 64 county files lose 83,970 homes against 612,170 of resident growth: replacement is 12.1% of the two together.

The HNA page now shows this figure as its own line under the need figure, with the need figure plus replacement beside it, in the Excel and PDF exports as well. It is not added to the headline tile or to the ranking’s future-pressure factor. Adding it would raise need most where housing is oldest. Because scores are percentiles, it would also move places with newer stock down. That choice belongs to the owner and has not been made. A place without its own projection ledger shows no replacement figure. The county total is not scaled down to it.

Not counted

  • Homes that stay standing but rise out of reach. Naturally occurring affordable housing, the private rentals cheap enough for lower-income households without a subsidy, can be lost to rent increases, renovation or sale. Nothing in the projection measures this. The ACS files the site stores hold only the current rent distribution, so no change over time can be computed from them. The loss can be large: the La Plata County Housing Needs Assessment (Root Policy Research, 24 October 2025, Section II, p. 11) reports that rental homes priced below $1,250 fell by more than a third from 2012 to 2022.
  • Income-restricted homes whose restrictions end. The HNA page lists each restricted property with its years to expiration, but no expiring home is added to need. The list is also incomplete: only 110 of 1,854 properties carry an end date. Of those, 77 have already passed it, and 431 homes reach theirs within ten years.
Read the figure as a floor

For every geography, the 20-year figure assumes that every home standing today, priced or restricted for lower-income households, is still standing and still affordable 20 years from now. Where housing is old, rents are rising or restrictions are ending, the need is larger than the figure says.

10 Why this method and not another

Each choice below has a widely used alternative. For each one this section says what the alternative does better, why it was not used here, and what the choice gives up. A claim about another method is taken from that method’s own documentation.

The rental gap: homes priced in the band, not homes available to it

The ranking’s housing gap compares renter households by income (ACS B25118) with occupied rental homes by gross rent (ACS B25063, homes paying cash rent), both placed into bands against HUD’s area median income. It counts every occupied home whose rent fits a band as supply for that band, whoever lives in it. Vacant rentals and homes rented without cash rent are not counted.

The main alternative is HUD’s affordable-and-available count, which the National Low Income Housing Coalition uses in The Gap. A home counts for a band only if its rent fits the band and it is vacant or rented by a household in the band. That is the better measure for the lowest incomes, because a cheap home held by a higher-income household is not available to a lower-income one. The difference is large. Summed over the 64 counties, each at its own area median income, the priced count finds 85,210 occupied rental homes at or below the 30% AMI rent. HUD finds 42,707 such homes rented by a household in that band, plus 3,087 vacant for rent: 45,794 affordable and available in all.

The ranking was built on the priced count before HUD’s place tables were in this repository, and it has not been rebuilt on them. The affordable-and-available count is shown on the home page and in the Deal Calculator. The two are not directly comparable: HUD’s tables are from the 2018–2022 ACS and use household-size-adjusted income limits, while the priced count uses the 2020–2024 ACS against the four-person limit. What the priced count gives up is accuracy at the bottom of the income range: it overstates the supply open to the lowest-income renters.

A second family of methods estimates underproduction: the homes a region would need for its households, plus the households that never formed, at a healthy vacancy rate, minus the homes it has. In its 2022 report, Up for Growth estimates it for all 309 metropolitan areas and for 505 non-metro regions of about 100,000 people, and Freddie Mac nationally. These estimates count homes missing from the stock, not who is short of an affordable one. Their smallest areas are also far larger than most Colorado towns. They answer a different question, and they cannot be computed for a place of a few thousand people.

Tract to place: the larger of two shares, not a block-level crosswalk

Cost burden is apportioned from tracts to places by the larger of area share and population share (§02). Area share alone, the earlier rule, undercounted a small town that held a sliver of a large rural tract’s land but most of its people. Crosswalks built from census blocks, such as Geocorr and NHGIS, weight by where people actually live and form a true partition. They are the sounder method, and they have not been adopted. There is also a simpler route: HUD publishes CHAS for places directly, and this repository now holds those tables for 482 places. Using them would remove the apportionment for those places. Until that is done, §8.3’s warning stands: the weights are not a partition, and place counts must not be summed.

The composite score: percentiles and published weights

Each factor enters as a percentile within its geography type, with fixed published weights (§06). The usual alternatives, set out in the OECD and European Commission Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators (2008), are z-scores, min–max scaling, and weights derived from the data by principal components or factor analysis. Z-scores keep the distance between places, which percentiles discard. Data-derived weights remove the analyst’s judgement from the weighting.

Percentiles were chosen because small-place rates are skewed and noisy. One town with a rate far from the rest moves every z-score in the pool, but it moves a percentile by one rank. The CDC’s Social Vulnerability Index also ranks places by percentile. Fixed weights were chosen because a council member can read them and argue with them. Weights from principal components change whenever the data are rebuilt, and it is hard to explain why a factor counts for what it does. The cost is the one §06 states: scores are positional, so a place cannot improve its score without improving relative to its peers, and the size of a difference is lost.

Ownership affordability: several models, not one index

The National Association of Realtors’ Housing Affordability Index asks whether a family on the median family income qualifies for the median-priced existing single-family home, with 20% down and principal and interest held to 25% of income. It is simple, long-running and comparable across the country. It is not used here because it answers the question for the median family only. A needs assessment asks what households at 60%, 80% or 100% of AMI can buy, with taxes, insurance and mortgage insurance in the payment. The 7 models in §05 answer that, at several lenders’ ratio conventions. The cost is that no single affordability number comes out of this system, and a reader has to choose a model.

Sampling error: what is measured, and what is not yet shown

Almost every small-area figure here comes from the American Community Survey, which is a sample. The Census Bureau publishes a margin of error with every estimate. Its handbook, Understanding and Using American Community Survey Data (2020), measures reliability by the coefficient of variation: the standard error, which is the 90% margin divided by 1.645, as a share of the estimate. It says there are no hard-and-fast rules for an acceptable error. A widely used convention is the one in the Washington Office of Financial Management’s ACS user guide: good at a CV of 15% or less, fair from 15% to 30%, and use with caution above 30%.

The errors at place level are large. For the 378 places with a published ACS 5-year rate (2020–2024), the median margin on the share of renters who are cost burdened is ±34.1 percentage points, and 280 places exceed ±20 points. At that size, a difference of a few points between two towns tells a reader nothing.

Read small-place rates as ranges

The HNA snapshot cards print each Census figure’s margin beside it, marked fair or use with caution when its coefficient of variation is above 15%, and name the year and release (1-year or 5-year) the figure came from. The second-homes panel uses the same scale; the cost-burden cross-check badge uses the margin but does not print it. Place pages, briefs and exports do not show margins yet. There, treat any rate for a place of a few thousand people as a range, and do not rank two small places against each other on a difference of a few points.
