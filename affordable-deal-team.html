Who Does What in a Colorado Affordable Deal

Bankers, syndicators, lawyers and community development · Rules checked 18 October 2026

An affordable apartment building is paid for by several groups of people who never meet the residents. Bankers turn the project into bonds that investors buy. Syndicators turn the federal tax credit into cash from investors, most of them banks. Lawyers write the agreements that hold it all together and keep the homes affordable for decades. And community development work, done in person, is what gets a town to say yes. This page explains each role, when it starts, and what rule creates it.

The short version

  • Investment bankers sell the tax-exempt bonds behind most larger deals. In Colorado, the Colorado Housing and Finance Authority (CHFA) usually issues those bonds, and each year's bond limit is split by state law between statewide issuers and local governments.
  • Syndicators raise cash from investors in exchange for the federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC), then watch over the property for the 15 years in which the credits can be taken back.
  • Lawyers decide the ownership structure, confirm the bonds are tax-exempt, and record the agreement that keeps rents affordable. Most structure has to be settled before the application, because deadlines run from the award.
  • Community development is the local work: meeting neighbors and officials, holding the hearings the law requires, and changing how the town talks about who will live there. CHFA asks every applicant to describe it.
  • Banks invest partly because the Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) gives them credit for it. Where that credit counts depends on the bank's assessment areas; a conservative and an expansive reading are below.

Investment bankers

Most larger affordable deals in Colorado use tax-exempt private activity bonds, which also unlock the "4 percent" tax credit. The investment banker's job is to sell those bonds. Three kinds of people do this work, and the rules treat them differently.

The underwriter is a buyer, not an advisor

An underwriter buys the bonds from the issuer and resells them to investors. The Municipal Securities Rulemaking Board (MSRB) requires the underwriter to tell the issuer plainly that it is on the other side of the deal.

"the underwriter's primary role is to purchase securities with a view to distribution … in an arm's-length commercial transaction with the issuer" and "unlike a municipal advisor, the underwriter does not have a fiduciary duty to the issuer."

Source: MSRB Rule G-17 and its interpretive notice on underwriter duties.

The municipal advisor owes the issuer loyalty

A municipal advisor is hired by the issuer to advise on the bonds and must put the issuer first.

A municipal advisor is "subject to a fiduciary duty that includes a duty of loyalty and a duty of care."

Source: MSRB Rule G-42.

Public sale, private placement or a bank loan

Bonds can be sold to the public, placed with a few sophisticated buyers by a placement agent, or bought directly by one bank. A placement agent sells "directly to investors on an agency basis rather than by purchasing the securities from the issuer and reselling them." Bonds sold in denominations of $100,000 or more to no more than 35 sophisticated buyers who are not buying to resell are exempt from the federal continuing-disclosure rule, which is one reason many affordable housing bonds are placed privately or with a single bank. For a pure placement agent, MSRB guidance notes the standard "arm's length" disclosure "may be inapplicable," because the agent acts for the issuer.

Sources: MSRB Glossary of Municipal Securities Terms (3rd ed.); SEC Rule 15c2-12(d)(1)(i); MSRB Rule G-17 interpretive notice.

Who issues the bonds in Colorado, and how much is allowed

Federal law caps private activity bonds in each state every year. Colorado's statute splits that cap: half to statewide authorities such as CHFA, and up to half to cities and counties in proportion to population, but only those large enough for a share of at least $1 million. CHFA puts the local share at 47 percent, with the other 3 percent held by the Department of Local Affairs (DOLA) in a statewide balance. Any local cap a city or county does not use by September 15 goes back to a statewide pool. A city or county can also give its share to CHFA to issue for a project in its area.

"Per statute: 50% to statewide authorities … 50% to local governments (greater than $1M, pro-rata by population) • Balance to DOLA." Local issuers may assign their cap: "option 1 to CHFA … option 2 to local issuer."

Sources: CHFA 2026 Private Activity Bond stakeholder session, December 4, 2025; CHFA PAB overview guide for local communities ("cities and counties - 47 percent"; "remaining volume cap (3 percent) is retained by the DOLA"); C.R.S. 24-32-1706 (unused local cap "shall automatically be relinquished to the statewide balance on September 15 of each year").

The 2025 change to the bond test

To earn the 4 percent credit on the whole building, bonds once had to finance at least half of the building and land. For buildings placed in service in tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, it can be 25 percent, if at least 5 percent of that cost is financed by bonds issued after that date. Less bond financing means each year's cap can reach more projects, which makes the banker's sizing of the bonds a bigger part of the plan. For new CHFA multifamily lending and 2026 Round Two projects, CHFA's policy is "Not more than 45% PAB financing, lower when feasible"; projects awarded earlier are unchanged.

Sources: 26 U.S.C. 42(h)(4)(B) and the effective-date note to Pub. L. 119-21, § 70422(b)(2); CHFA 2026 Private Activity Bond stakeholder session (above).

Syndicators

The federal tax credit is claimed over ten years by whoever owns the building. A developer usually cannot use that much tax savings, so a syndicator brings in investors who can, and they pay cash up front that becomes part of the money that builds the homes.

What they do

Syndicators "help connect investors" with developers and "oversee the fund's acquisition and the ongoing management of the projects." They run funds for one investor or for many.

Source: GAO-17-285R, Low-Income Housing Tax Credit: The Role of Syndicators.

How the ownership works

Investors pool money in an upper partnership that owns the property partnerships below it. "Investors hold 99.99 percent … the syndicator, as general partner or managing member, holds 0.01 percent" of the fund.

Source: OCC Community Developments Insights, LIHTCs: Affordable Housing Investment Opportunities for Banks.

How they are paid

"an initial acquisition fee—usually a percentage of the gross equity raised," plus an annual asset management fee.

Source: GAO-17-285R (above).

Why they watch for 15 years

If the building stops qualifying during the 15-year compliance period, the investor's tax "shall be increased by the credit recapture amount." The syndicator's oversight protects against that.

Source: 26 U.S.C. 42(i)(1) and 42(j)(1).

What the developer negotiates with the syndicator

  • Price: cents of cash per dollar of credit. Current benchmarks are on Tax Credit Equity Markets.
  • Pay-in schedule: how much cash arrives at closing, at completion and at stabilization. Developers "prefer to receive equity contributions earlier."
  • Guaranties: agreements "specify developer guarantees, fee schedules, and reserve requirements … completion, operating, and tax delivery guarantees."
  • Adjusters: investors "can negotiate so-called tax credit adjusters … so investors can reduce their anticipated capital contributions" if fewer credits arrive than promised.
  • The exit: after year 15, "The general partner's purchase of the limited partner investors' interest is the most common form of investor exit."

Source: OCC Community Developments Insights (above).

Lawyers and deal structure

A tax credit deal is a set of contracts and recorded covenants that must satisfy federal tax law, CHFA, lenders and investors at the same time. The lawyers make those fit, and most of the important choices cannot be changed after the award.

Why structure comes first

  • The clock starts at the award. For a 9 percent award, CHFA requires a carryover or final application "within 13 months of Preliminary Reservation," and buildings "must place in service no later than 24 months after the calendar year" of the carryover. Federal law also requires more than 10 percent of the expected cost to be incurred within a year of a carryover allocation. A 4 percent bond deal has no carryover step, but it has its own clock. CHFA's "Initial Determination" stays valid "through the end of the third year after the issuance of the Determination Letter," and a project not placed in service by then is re-checked against the current plan. Once a building opens, CHFA must be told "within 15 days," placed-in-service documents are due "within 45 days," and the rest of the Final Allocation materials within six months, with a late fee after that. Either way, a structure still being argued after the award costs time the schedule does not have.
  • Ownership decides taxes. When a housing authority holds an ownership share, Colorado exempts the project from property tax, and from sales and use tax during construction, in proportion to its low-income share (C.R.S. 29-4-227). Home-rule cities that collect their own sales tax may not honor the construction exemption, so ask the city before budgeting; the state's guidance "does not apply to sales taxes administered directly by home rule cities" (Colorado Department of Revenue). That share has to be in the ownership structure from the start.
  • Ownership decides the exit. Who holds a right to buy the property after year 15 is written into the partnership agreement at closing.

Sources: CHFA 2025–2026 Qualified Allocation Plan, Second Amendment; 26 U.S.C. 42(h)(1)(E).

The agreement that keeps it affordable

Federal law requires a long-term use agreement "binding on all successors of the taxpayer" and "recorded pursuant to State law as a restrictive covenant." In Colorado that is CHFA's Land Use Restriction Agreement: once placed-in-service documents are in, "a LURA will be executed and recorded."

Federal law lets an owner ask CHFA, after year 14, to find a buyer through a "qualified contract." The buyer has to keep the homes affordable; only if CHFA cannot present such a buyer within a year can the long-term restriction end early, and even then existing tenants keep three years of protection against eviction and rent increases. In Colorado this route is mostly closed. CHFA's plan says its qualified contract provisions "only apply to projects that received awards of Housing Tax Credits prior to 2019," and that the process "is available to very few projects in Colorado as most Owners waived their rights to pursue this option under their Land Use Restriction Agreement." For those that remain, "CHFA will not consider a Qualified Contract Request until the Owner secures a complete, unconditional waiver of all purchase options, including a nonprofit general partner's right of first refusal." A nonprofit or government partner's right to buy after year 15, at a price set in federal law, is one of the main ways homes stay affordable, which is why it is negotiated at the start.

Sources: 26 U.S.C. 42(h)(6)(E)–(F) and 42(i)(7); CHFA 2025–2026 Qualified Allocation Plan, Second Amendment.

Community development: getting everyone on board

This is the part no spreadsheet does. Someone has to walk the neighborhood, sit with the council, the school district, the employers and the neighbors, hear what worries them, and come back with answers. It starts before the site is chosen, because the questions people ask shape the project.

Where the rules require local voice

  • Every tax credit project: federal law requires the allocating agency to notify "the chief executive officer (or the equivalent) of the local jurisdiction" and give a "reasonable opportunity to comment." CHFA also notifies the "city or county manager, city council member or county commissioner, and local housing authority if applicable" and "will consider any comments received prior to the award decision."
  • The application itself: CHFA's narrative "must provide a description of outreach to the community and how the Application contributes to promoting equity and economic mobility for residents."
  • Colorado state credits: the applicant "must have conducted a Public Hearing in the community," and must show "that local government will provide some monetary, in-kind, or other contribution benefitting the proposed project."
  • Points for local plans: CHFA awards points to projects in a community with "an identified community affordable housing priority," with evidence "the project fits into the community's need."
  • Bond deals: the public hearing and elected-official approval under 26 U.S.C. 147(f).

Sources: 26 U.S.C. 42(m)(1)(A)(ii); CHFA 2025–2026 QAP, Second Amendment (sections 3.B.5, Thresholds 15 and 16, Project Location scoring); CHFA State Credit Public Hearing Guidance.

Changing the words a town uses

How housing is described changes whether people support it. In survey experiments run by the FrameWorks Institute with Enterprise Community Partners, describing the problem as housing cost and availability "produced statistically significant increases on the salience of housing as a political priority and people's sense of collective responsibility," while a housing insecurity frame "had no effect." The researchers' advice: "by affirmatively explaining the positive effects of addressing affordability issues, communicators can build greater support." A 2025 study in the Journal of Experimental Political Science found that "correcting stereotypes about affordable housing and misperceptions about its effects increase support for affordable housing," and that the effect is often larger for housing near the respondent's own home.

In practice that means talking about the people and the jobs: the teacher, the nurse, the bus driver, the grown child who cannot afford to move back. It works best when those people, or their employers, say it themselves. A school district or hospital that needs the homes is a more convincing messenger than the developer who will build them.

Sources: FrameWorks Institute, Finding a Frame for Affordable Housing (2018); "Debunking NIMBY Myths Increases Support for Affordable Housing," Journal of Experimental Political Science (2025).

What has worked in Colorado

  • Voters said yes when the homes were named for the workers. In November 2022, CHFA counted "27 local affordable housing ballot measures … 21 of which passed," including Eagle County's "Lodging Tax to Support Local Workforce" and Glenwood Springs' "Accommodations Tax for Workforce Housing."
  • School districts as partners. Summit School District transferred "the 10-acre McCain Property to the Town" of Breckenridge for "35 deed-restricted homes … prioritized for SSD staff through a lottery." Durango School District 9-R bought the 35-unit Lightner Creek Apartments; its operations chief said "Dedicated, attainable housing is the gap we need to fill so they can live, work and build their lives here."

Sources: CHFA, November 2022 election results; Summit School District, June 2025; Mile High CRE, November 13, 2025.

Showing the benefits with local numbers

  • Who is struggling now. Each place's Housing Needs Assessment shows how many renters pay more than 30 and 50 percent of income for housing, and how many homes are short at each income level. CHFA scores applications on the same kind of cost-burden data, so it is the evidence reviewers already use.
  • Jobs and spending. CHFA's 2026 statewide economic profile credits the rental housing it helped finance from 1974 to 2025 with 148,961 jobs supported. These are CHFA's own model estimates ("Economic impact assessments made by CHFA using IMPLAN"), not counted jobs, so present them that way.
  • Neighbors' home values. The most-cited study, published in the Journal of Political Economy in 2019, found nearby home prices rose about 6.5 percent after tax credit housing was built in low-income areas, and fell about 2.5 percent in higher-income areas. Say both when the question comes up.

Sources: CHFA Statewide Economic Profile 2026; University of Colorado Real Estate Center summary of Diamond and McQuade (2019).

When the town itself will own or help run the homes, a staffed housing authority is often the local partner that carries this work for decades. A Colorado city or town forms one under C.R.S. 29-4-204, a county under C.R.S. 29-4-503, and two or more governments together under C.R.S. 29-1-204.5. The steps, powers and board choices are laid out in Starting a Housing Authority in Colorado.

Which banks have CRA reasons to invest

Banks are the largest buyers of tax credits, and the Community Reinvestment Act is a large part of why: "An important incentive for banks investing in LIHTCs is the CRA consideration" (OCC). A bank's examiners give that credit only for investments that benefit certain places. So the useful question for a Colorado project is: which banks get CRA credit for investing here?

The rules in force today

Every bank is examined under the 1995 CRA regulations. The 2023 rewrite was stopped by a federal court before it took effect; the OCC and FDIC have proposed a different update (published August 12, 2026, 91 FR 52114; comments closed October 13, 2026) that the Federal Reserve did not join, and no final rule has been published. Only large banks are graded on an investment test. For 2026 a bank is large if it had $1.649 billion or more in assets on December 31 of both 2024 and 2025; a bank below that on either date is still a small bank. On that test a tax credit fund investment can count as a "qualified investment" if its primary purpose is community development. More on the pending rules is on CRA Expansion Impact on LIHTC Pricing.

Sources: Mayer Brown, August 14, 2026 (the proposal, its date, and that the Federal Reserve "will continue to apply the 1995 regulations"); Federal Reserve, FDIC and OCC, 2026 asset-size thresholds, December 30, 2025.

Conservative: the bank's own assessment areas

The counties, or parts of counties, a bank has delineated and its examiner accepted. They must "include the geographies in which the bank has its main office, its branches, and its deposit-taking ATMs." A project inside a bank's assessment area is in the right place for CRA credit; whether the investment counts still depends on its primary purpose being community development, and the bank's examiner decides. A bank with a branch in the county is the first call, once you have checked that the project's census tract falls inside its area.

Source: 12 CFR 228.41(c) and 228.12(t) (a "qualified investment" must have "as its primary purpose community development"; Federal Reserve, text as of March 29, 2024, which examiners still apply; the current eCFR shows the enjoined 2023 rule. The OCC and FDIC rules at 12 CFR 25 and 345 read the same).

Expansive: the broader statewide or regional area

The investment test credits "qualified investments that benefit its assessment area(s) or a broader statewide or regional area that includes the bank's assessment area(s)." For a bank with any Colorado assessment area, a project elsewhere in Colorado can count, but only if its primary purpose is community development and the bank has met its home areas' needs first: the agencies' guidance says examiners consider such activity "as long as the institution has been responsive to community development needs and opportunities in its assessment area(s)." Wholesale and limited-purpose banks can get credit for activity outside their areas "if the bank has adequately addressed the needs of its assessment area(s)." A bank's latest performance evaluation says how its examiners judged that, so read it before pitching an out-of-area investment.

Sources: 12 CFR 228.23(a) and 228.25(e); Interagency Questions and Answers Regarding Community Reinvestment, Q&A §__.12(h)–6.

Where to look up any bank

  • Its assessment areas and past tax credit investments: the bank's latest CRA performance evaluation, through the FFIEC Interagency CRA Rating Search, or the agency's own search: FDIC, Federal Reserve, or OCC. Evaluations come every few years, so they can be dated.
  • Its current branches by county: the FDIC's Summary of Deposits, updated each year with branches as of June 30. Because assessment areas must include branch locations, a branch in your county makes a bank a likely candidate. It is a screen, not proof: a bank may draw its assessment area around only part of a county, so confirm that the project's census tract is inside it using the bank's performance evaluation or public file.
  • Its public file: every bank must keep one with its latest evaluation and "A list of the bank's branches, their street addresses, and census tracts" (12 CFR 228.43). Ask the bank for it.
