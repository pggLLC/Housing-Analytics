What makes LIHTC deals actually hard

The tax credit program is the most effective affordable housing production tool in U.S. history — and one of the most technically demanding. Here is what the Deal Calculator’s pro forma does not show you.

Credit pricing is a market — and it moves

The equity price per dollar of tax credit (the "credit price") is determined by what tax credit investors will pay. For 2026-Q2, Novogradac's national averages were $0.82 for 9% credits and $0.83 for 4% credits, and its Colorado median (all credit types) was $0.81 (Novogradac LIHTC equity pricing trends; regional and state pricing). A 10-cent swing on a $1M annual credit changes your equity by $1M. LIHTC equity closes 12–18 months after allocation, during which your project's financial structure is locked — but market conditions are not.

Eligible basis is not total development cost

Only "residential" costs qualify: construction, some soft costs, architect fees, developer overhead up to a limit. Land, commercial space, excess developer fee, and many financing costs are excluded. HUD's basis boost (130% in QCTs and DDAs) can add meaningful credit but requires specific HUD certification. The QCT/DDA overlay on the Housing Needs Assessment map shows your project's eligibility.

9% credits are competitive — not guaranteed

CHFA allocates 9% credits through a competitive QAP process. For 2026 each state's 9% ceiling is $3.416 per resident (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32), about $20.5 million for Colorado. A typical 60-unit LIHTC project needs $1.2–$1.8M in annual credits. Demand routinely exceeds supply by 3:1 or more. Applications score on: site control, readiness, community support, income targeting, green standards, nonprofit involvement, and design quality. Rejection does not mean the project is bad — it often means it is early.

Design for livability before you draw the first plan

This calculator does not price design, because no study supports a dollar value for a courtyard or a community room. The design choices still matter to residents and to CHFA, and most of them are cheap only at the start:

  • One step-free route from the sidewalk and parking to every entrance, unit and shared room, with controls and fixtures anyone can use.
  • Units with the same amenities and square footage for every income tier, spread through the building, as the QAP asks.
  • Shade trees and water-wise planting instead of turf, with outdoor space residents can reach and use.
  • Small homes that work: storage inside where it is used, a defined entry, rooms checked against real furniture, and a porch deep enough to sit on.
  • Shared rooms on the routes residents already walk, bedrooms that fit the households, and quiet between units.
  • Noisy rooms kept away from bedrooms and routes that are easy to follow, and in supportive housing, a design shaped around residents' safety and choice.

Read the livability brief for the evidence and for what CHFA requires, scores and rewards.

The "gap" problem is structural

LIHTC rents are capped below market to serve low-income residents — which is the point. But lower revenue means lower debt capacity. The gap between total development cost and available financing (tax credit equity + first mortgage) is filled by: CHFA subordinate loans, HOME funds, CDBG, local housing trust funds, seller-carry notes, and deferred developer fee. Stacking these sources requires coordinated closings and subordination agreements. A typical 60-unit deal has 5–8 financing sources.

Compliance runs 30+ years

The initial compliance period is 15 years. The extended use period adds another 15 (30 total). During this time, units must be rent-restricted and income-qualified. Annual CHFA compliance audits, IRS Form 8609, tenant income certifications (TICs), and annual owner's certificates are all required. A compliance violation can trigger credit recapture — the investor gets back the credits they claimed, plus interest and penalties. This liability runs through the full compliance period.

Services need their own budget

The Deal Calculator’s operating expense figure is a general per-unit default with no separate line for resident or supportive services. If you plan a services coordinator, a supportive housing set-aside or CHFA's homelessness and special-populations points, price the services each year and name who pays for each year: operations, a capitalized reserve, the developer fee boost CHFA requires to be spent on services or rent help, or an outside funder. Buildings are financed for decades; services money is usually renewed one year at a time. How services help, what CHFA requires, and what they cost.

Working with banks: CRA and the capital stack

Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) obligations incentivize banks to invest in affordable housing — either as LIHTC equity investors, construction lenders, or permanent lenders. Banks under CRA examination pressure are often the best sources of below-market subordinate debt. Identifying your local CRA lenders early — before allocation — can open financing that is not available through standard channels. See the lender guide below.
